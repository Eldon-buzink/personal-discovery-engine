A Holland Code sorts occupational interest into six types — Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional (RIASEC) — based on the idea, first proposed by psychologist John Holland in 1959, that people and work environments both have a "personality," and satisfaction tracks how well the two match.

## The six types

- **Realistic** ("Making things work") — working with tools, machines, or physical materials, wanting a tangible result
- **Investigative** ("Figuring things out") — digging into why something happens, research, open questions
- **Artistic** ("Making something original") — creative freedom, self-expression, making something that didn't exist before
- **Social** ("Helping people grow") — helping, teaching, or supporting other people directly
- **Enterprising** ("Making things happen") — leading, pitching, persuading, competing for an outcome
- **Conventional** ("Keeping things organized") — order, accuracy, systems that run smoothly

Most career quizzes stop at the RIASEC letters themselves — a "you're an IAS" result with no plain-language translation. Bearing scores the same six types but reports them under the plain-language names above, built for the result screen, not the psychology textbook.

## Why a ranked list instead of a forced three-letter code

The traditional Holland Code format picks your top 3 types and orders them into a code like "IAS," regardless of how the actual numbers landed. That's a real information loss: someone who scores 4.6 on Investigative, 4.5 on Artistic, 4.4 on Social, and 4.3 on Enterprising has four genuinely close contenders, while someone who scores 4.8 on Investigative and then drops to 3.1 on everything else has exactly one dominant type. A fixed top-3 code treats both people identically.

Bearing's Direction branch scores all 6 types from 24 items — 4 per type, direct interest statements ("I like digging into a problem until I really understand why it happens"), no reverse-scoring — and keeps the full ranking rather than truncating it internally. What gets shown is 1 to 3 types: your top-ranked type always appears as your closest match, and the next-ranked types only get surfaced if their score is actually close enough to count as a real contender, not just whatever happened to be third. Someone with one clearly dominant type sees one type. Someone with several closely bunched types sees the bunching itself reflected in the result, instead of an arbitrary code that implies three equally strong interests where there might only be one.

## What it isn't

A Holland Code isn't a career prescription and it isn't a personality trait score. It describes interest content — what kind of work holds your attention — not ability, not personality style, and not how well you'd actually perform in a given role. Someone can rank high in Enterprising and still be a poor fit for a specific sales job for reasons the interest ranking alone can't capture (working style, energy patterns, or plain lack of relevant skill). It's one input, read alongside — not in place of — the rest of a fuller picture.
