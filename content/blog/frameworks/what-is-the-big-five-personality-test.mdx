The Big Five personality test measures five broad dimensions of personality — Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism — each on a continuous scale rather than sorting you into a fixed type. It's the model with the strongest and most consistent research backing of any personality framework in wide use, and the one Bearing's own assessment is built on.

## The five domains, and the thirty facets under them

Each domain breaks into six narrower facets — 5 × 6 = 30 total — and this facet layer is where the model's actual detail lives. A single Extraversion score, for instance, blends six separate things that don't always move together in one person:

- **Openness** — Imagination, Artistic Interests, Emotionality, Adventurousness, Intellect, Liberalism
- **Conscientiousness** — Self-Efficacy, Orderliness, Dutifulness, Achievement-Striving, Self-Discipline, Cautiousness
- **Extraversion** — Friendliness, Gregariousness, Assertiveness, Activity Level, Excitement-Seeking, Cheerfulness
- **Agreeableness** — Trust, Morality, Altruism, Cooperation, Modesty, Sympathy
- **Neuroticism** — Anxiety, Anger, Depression, Self-Consciousness, Immoderation, Vulnerability

Two people can land in an identical place on Extraversion overall while looking almost nothing alike day to day — one driven by Gregariousness, the other by Assertiveness — which is exactly why Bearing's report shows all 30 facets individually rather than five blended domain scores.

## How a raw score becomes a result

This is the part most personality tests handle differently, and it's worth being specific about how Bearing does it rather than describing the framework only in the abstract. Each facet is scored from four IPIP-NEO items on a 1–5 scale, averaged, then sorted into one of three bands: **3.5 or higher reads as high, 2.5 to 3.49 reads as mid, and anything below 2.5 reads as low.**

The bands aren't just labels on a number line — each carries a specific meaning. High and low both predict something consistent (the trait shows up strongly, or its opposite shows up instead, across most situations). Mid specifically predicts something different: that the trait shows up *situationally* rather than consistently in either direction — present in contexts that pull for it, absent otherwise. That's a different claim than "moderate," even though the number sits in the middle.

Bearing then attaches an actual word to each band, per facet — not a generic "high/medium/low," but a specific term chosen for what that combination measures. A high Anxiety score reads as "Anxious," mid as "Attuned," low as "Grounded." A high Trust score reads as "Trusting," mid as "Discerning," low as "Vigilant." Thirty facets, three bands each, ninety distinct words — a level of specificity that goes beyond what the underlying Big Five framework itself requires, since the five-domain, thirty-facet structure is public-domain research, not something any one product owns.

## Where the framework comes from

The Big Five wasn't invented by one researcher or one company. It emerged from decades of independent factor-analytic studies converging on the same five-dimension structure. Psychologists Robert McCrae and Paul Costa formalized one influential version in the NEO-PI-R. Separately, Lewis Goldberg led the [International Personality Item Pool](https://ipip.ori.org/) project, an open-source item bank that produced the IPIP-NEO — the version Bearing's assessment is built from, specifically the 120-item, 4-item-per-facet version. The [APA's own definition of the model](https://dictionary.apa.org/big-five-personality-model) reflects the same five-dimension structure, identified consistently across independent lines of research.

## What it isn't

The Big Five is not a typing system. There's no result that sorts you into one of 16 boxes, no type you get assigned and identify with afterward. Every dimension is continuous, and most people score in the mid-range of most facets rather than at either extreme — which, per the scoring logic above, doesn't mean "unremarkable." It means situational. [Adventurousness](/blog/adventurousness), one of the 30 facets under Openness, is a concrete example of what that facet-level, band-specific detail looks like in practice.
