Both frameworks claim to measure personality. They disagree on almost everything else — what a result is, how stable it's supposed to be, and what a middling score even means.

## What each one is actually built from

The Big Five wasn't proposed by one person. It's the product of decades of independent factor-analytic research converging, repeatedly, on the same five-dimension structure — a finding that kept surviving re-tests rather than being asserted once and left alone. MBTI runs the opposite direction: Katharine Briggs and Isabel Myers built it directly from Carl Jung's theory of psychological types, a fixed framework applied to people rather than a structure derived from how people's answers actually cluster. One model was discovered by repeatedly checking; the other was designed and then applied.

## A continuous score vs. a forced binary

This is the mechanical difference that drives most of the practical ones. MBTI's four dichotomies (e.g., Introversion/Extraversion) split every respondent into exactly one of two boxes — there's no result that says "close to the middle." The Big Five scores each dimension on a continuous scale instead, and Bearing's own facet scoring makes the consequence of that concrete: a mid-range score isn't rounded up or down into a category, it's read as its own distinct result, specifically predicting that the trait shows up *situationally* — present in some contexts, absent in others — rather than consistently in either direction. MBTI has no equivalent output. Someone who's genuinely near the middle on Introversion/Extraversion still gets handed one letter, because the framework has no third option to hand them instead.

## Why retaking MBTI so often changes the result

Research on MBTI's psychometric properties (Pittenger, 2005) has repeatedly found weak type-retest reliability — a meaningful share of people who retake it land in a different four-letter type entirely. That's not really a measurement bug so much as a predictable consequence of forcing a binary split on traits that mostly don't distribute as two separate humps: most people sit closer to the middle of most dimensions than to either extreme, which means small shifts in mood or mindset when answering are enough to tip a near-50/50 respondent across the line. A continuous score doesn't have a line to tip across — landing near the midpoint is simply the result, stable across retests in a way a forced letter can't be.

## What this means for a result you're actually going to use

A binary type is easy to remember and easy to share — that's a real part of MBTI's popularity, not a coincidence. But it costs you the exact information a mid-range score is built to carry: that a trait is context-dependent rather than absent or muted. Bearing's report keeps that distinction because "this shows up sometimes" and "this doesn't show up" are different, useful, separately actionable pieces of information — collapsing them into one convenient letter isn't a simplification, it's a loss.
