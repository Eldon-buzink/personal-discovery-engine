The IPIP-NEO-120 is a specific, named instrument — 120 items, 4 per facet, across the Big Five's 30 facets — not a generic synonym for "a Big Five test." It's the exact instrument Bearing's assessment runs, and knowing what it actually is (where it came from, what it trades off, what it isn't) is different from knowing the Big Five model in the abstract.

## Why a public-domain version needed to exist

By the 1990s, the five-factor model had a well-validated commercial instrument in the NEO-PI-R, developed by Robert McCrae and Paul Costa — but no equivalent anyone could use without a license. That gap is specifically what the International Personality Item Pool was built to close. Lewis Goldberg, at the Oregon Research Institute, started the IPIP project in the mid-1990s as a public-domain bank of personality items, free for any use, designed to measure the same well-established five-domain, thirty-facet structure without requiring a commercial license. The IPIP-NEO-120 is a direct, purpose-built descendant of that project: 4 items per facet, 30 facets, developed specifically as a shorter, freely available alternative validated against the commercial original rather than derived from it.

## What "4 items per facet" actually trades off

The full IPIP-NEO runs up to 300 items — 10 per facet — for researchers who need the strongest possible per-facet reliability. The 120-item version cuts that to 4 items per facet, which trades some statistical precision for a completion time closer to what an actual person will sit through. That tradeoff matters more for research applications comparing small effect sizes across large samples than it does for an individual taking the assessment once: 4 items per facet is enough to place someone reliably into a high/mid/low band, which is the resolution Bearing's report actually reports at.

## How this is different from "a Big Five test" generically

Plenty of free online quizzes claim to measure "the Big Five" without specifying which underlying item set they're actually built from — some use short, unvalidated custom item sets that happen to output five familiar-sounding trait names. The IPIP-NEO-120 is a specific, named, validated instrument with a public research record behind it, not an approximation built to resemble one. Bearing's report is built on this exact instrument, scored at the facet level (30 individual scores) rather than collapsed into five blended domain numbers — which is also why Bearing's "where this shows up" specificity per facet is possible: it's reading from 30 distinct measurements, not backfilling detail onto 5.
