A four-letter type from a test taken in a college dorm room a decade ago still shows up in dating profiles and Slack bios today, cited like a fixed fact. That's an odd way to treat a measurement of something that, by most of the actual research, shifts over the course of a life — sometimes gradually, sometimes in response to a specific stretch of years that changed how someone operates.

Trait stability is real, and it's higher than people expect — someone in the top third of a trait at twenty-five is more likely than not to still be in the top third at forty-five. But "more likely than not" is a statement about a population, not a promise about any one person, and it leaves real room for individual change that a single score from a decade ago can't capture. A stressful decade, a role that demanded something different, a relationship that reshaped how someone handles conflict — any of these can move a score, and none of them show up if the only data point is one test taken once.

## What a single score actually captures

A one-time result is a snapshot, and it's honest about being one only if you keep treating it that way. The problem isn't taking the test — it's the quiet drift from "here's what this measured at the time" to "this is who I am," which happens almost automatically once a result gets repeated enough times, especially if it comes with a memorable label attached.

## What retesting actually shows you

The more useful frame is to treat a single score as the start of a record, not the end of one. Retaking an assessment a year or two later doesn't just refresh the number — it tells you something the first test alone couldn't: whether a specific score was a stable pattern or a temporary reflection of whatever was happening in your life at the time. A trait that moved is at least as informative as one that didn't, and neither is visible from a single measurement.
