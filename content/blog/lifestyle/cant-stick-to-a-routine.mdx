**A routine is a task you have to start again every single day.** A one-off task, like filing a form, only needs one good start. A routine needs a new start each morning, whether or not you feel like it. That is the main reason routines are harder to keep than plans are to make.

Someone lays out their running shoes the night before. They mean it. The next morning the shoes are right there, the plan hasn't changed, and they still don't put them on. Most people blame laziness. But they chose this routine and they still want what it gives them. The wanting is there. What's missing is the start.

## Why day four is the hard day

The first days of a new routine are carried by the fun of something new. You're curious, you're proud of yourself, and that pushes you out the door. Around day three to five, that feeling runs out. Now the routine has to start on its own, with nothing extra pushing it.

This is also where people who find starting hard notice it most. In Bearing's model, that is the [Self-Discipline](/blog/self-discipline) facet: how much push you need to begin something you've already decided to do. If you score low on it, every morning costs a bit more. The routine itself might be easy once you're in it. Getting in is the expensive part, and a routine makes you pay it every day.

## The missed day that ends it

Most routines don't die slowly. They die after one missed day, because the missed day turns into a story: "I broke the streak, so it's over." The next morning the start feels even heavier, because now it also means admitting the slip.

A missed day costs very little on its own. Two missed days in a row is where a routine usually stops being one. So the rule that helps most is simple: never miss twice. After a bad day, do the tiniest version of the routine the next day. Five minutes of a run still counts, because it keeps the habit of starting alive.

## What keeps a routine going

These target the daily start, which is where routines break:

- **Tie it to something you already do.** "After I make coffee, I put on my shoes" gives the start a cue. It no longer depends on remembering or on how you feel.
- **Make the first step tiny.** Aim for something you could do on your worst morning, like putting on the shoes, not "go for a 5k run." Once you've started, carrying on is much easier.
- **Plan the bad-day version in advance.** Decide now what the two-minute version is, so on a hard day you don't have to decide anything.
- **Count starts, not results.** Tick the day off if you began. Showing up every day matters more than how far you got.

If you think about the plan too much before you begin, that's a different thing. It's closer to [Cautiousness](/blog/cautiousness), which is about the thinking you do before you decide. A routine can be well planned and still fail every morning at the start.
