Someone lays out running shoes the night before with real sincerity. Morning comes, the shoes are right there, nothing about the plan has changed — and they still don't get put on. That specific gap, between a decision that was genuinely made and an action that was supposed to follow it, is where a broken routine actually lives. It isn't in the planning, and it usually isn't in the wanting.

## Not laziness, and not overthinking

Two explanations get reached for automatically, and both usually miss. "I'm lazy" implies not wanting to do the thing — but most people who can't sustain a routine do want to; they chose it, they value the outcome, and they still don't do it, which laziness doesn't explain. "I overthink everything" is the second guess, and it's a different mechanism than the one actually at fault: overthinking-before-deciding and struggling-to-start-after-deciding are two separate facets doing two separate jobs, and it's specifically the second one showing up here, not the first.

## What's actually happening at the moment it breaks

[Self-Discipline](/blog/self-discipline) is the facet that measures that specific gap — the friction between a decision already made and the action that's supposed to follow it, how much activation energy it takes to begin, not whether the desire is there. It's a different question from [Cautiousness](/blog/cautiousness), which is about the thinking that happens *before* a decision: planning ahead, weighing options, not committing hastily. Someone can be high in Cautiousness and low in Self-Discipline at once — they plan the routine carefully, choose a sensible version of it, commit to starting Monday — and then still don't get up when the alarm goes off, because the planning was never where the problem lived. The shoes-by-the-door moment reopens fresh every single day, regardless of how firm yesterday's resolve was.

## Why the usual fixes target the wrong thing

Since the friction is specifically about starting, not about wanting or planning, most fixes people reach for miss the actual barrier. A stricter schedule doesn't reduce activation energy — it just adds another thing to not-start. What actually shrinks the barrier is making the first action itself smaller: shoes by the door, not "go for a run." That targets the exact point where the gap opens, instead of reinforcing a plan that was never the weak link to begin with.
