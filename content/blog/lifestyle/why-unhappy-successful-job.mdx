Success, in most workplaces, is measured by output and evaluation — performance reviews, promotions, whether the work gets done well and on time. None of that measures whether the work itself is a good match for you. Those are different questions, scored by different things, and a person can score well on the first while quietly failing the second for months before it's obvious why.

## Two separate questions, one word

"Fit" gets used to cover both "can you do this well" and "does this work actually suit you," as if a yes to the first implies a yes to the second. Bearing's report keeps them structurally apart because they don't move together. The Energy branch, adapted from Self-Determination Theory, ranks what fuels you and what drains you across three needs — Autonomy, Competence, and Relatedness — split into their own fuel-framed and drain-framed item sets. The Direction branch, separately, ranks which of six interest types (Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional) actually holds your attention. Neither branch asks anything about performance or ability. A role can score you highly on competence — you're objectively good at it — while still structurally starving your top fuel category or sitting nowhere near your top-ranked Direction type, and none of that shows up on a review.

## What the mismatch actually feels like

It rarely announces itself as "wrong job." It shows up as something quieter and easier to misdiagnose: consistent low-grade dread before a task you're objectively good at, or relief that's disproportionate to how hard the day actually was. If your top fuel is Autonomy (energized by deciding how to do something yourself) and your role is heavily prescribed — clear steps, little discretion — the drain isn't about workload. It's specifically that the day-to-day keeps hitting your drain category ("Feeling boxed in") regardless of how well you execute inside those constraints. Someone whose top fuel is Relatedness can hit the identical pattern in a role that's technically collaborative on paper but low on the kind of real connection ("A real conversation, not small talk") that actually fuels them — lots of meetings, little of what the category is actually asking for.

## Why this isn't just burnout

Burnout is usually described as depletion from sustained overload — too much, for too long. A structural mismatch between your fuel/drain ranking (or your Direction interests) and your actual day-to-day can produce a similar unhappiness at a perfectly sustainable workload, because the problem isn't quantity, it's kind. Two people in the same role, working the same hours, can have completely different experiences of it depending on whether the work's actual content lines up with what independently fuels them — which is also why generic advice ("set better boundaries," "take more breaks") often doesn't touch this particular version of the problem. The hours aren't the issue.

## What's actually worth checking

Not "am I good at this" — that question is usually already answered, and answering it again doesn't explain the dread. The more useful pair: does the day-to-day actually supply what your top fuel category needs, and does the work's content sit anywhere near your top-ranked Direction interest? Both are measurable independently of performance, and both can be true-successful, false-satisfied at the same time.
