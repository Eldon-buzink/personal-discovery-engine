An attachment style quiz measures two things — anxiety about whether a close relationship is secure, and avoidance of closeness itself — and maps the combination to a pattern. That part is well established, adapted from real attachment research (the ECR-R instrument). What most standalone quizzes skip is the second half of the question: whether that pattern is typical or surprising given everything else true about the person taking it.

## What a typical attachment quiz gives you

Most free attachment quizzes online exist to produce a shareable label fast — anxious, avoidant, secure, or some four-box variant — from a short item set, often without clearly separating the two underlying dimensions before scoring. That's not necessarily inaccurate, but it's incomplete by construction: a label alone doesn't tell you whether it's the expected reading for someone like you, or a genuine outlier worth paying closer attention to.

## The comparison a standalone quiz can't make

Here's the gap concretely. [Trust](/blog/trust), a Big Five Agreeableness facet, measures how readily someone extends good faith to people in general. [Anxiety](/blog/anxiety), a Neuroticism facet, measures how much someone worries across situations broadly — not attachment-specific fear of losing a connection. Both are close cousins to attachment anxiety and avoidance, but they're not the same measurement, and they don't always move together in the same person. Someone can score as generally trusting and generally low-anxiety on the Big Five side and *still* carry an anxious or avoidant relationship pattern — which is a more specific and more useful thing to know than the pattern alone, because it tells you the pattern isn't just an extension of an already-anxious or already-guarded personality. It's something narrower, tied specifically to closeness.

A standalone attachment quiz has no facet scores to check itself against. It can hand you a pattern; it can't tell you whether that pattern lines up with or diverges from the rest of your personality.

## How Bearing scores it instead

Bearing's Relationships branch runs 15 items adapted from the ECR-R, scoring anxiety and avoidance as two independent dimensions rather than collapsing straight to a single label, and maps the combination to one of four named patterns. The full breakdown of those four patterns — and what "attachment style" actually predicts about who you're drawn to — is covered in [why you might keep dating the same type of person](/blog/dating-the-same-type-of-person). What matters here is that this score sits in the same report as your Trust and Anxiety facets, not off in a separate quiz with nothing to compare itself to — so the result you get is "here's your pattern, and here's how it relates to the rest of what's already been measured," not just a label on its own.

## What to actually do with the result

An attachment pattern isn't fixed for life, but it also doesn't move just from reading a label. What the underlying research points to as actually changing it: a relationship that consistently doesn't confirm the old pattern, sustained over real time, or therapy aimed specifically at the pattern rather than at any one relationship. Knowing the pattern — and knowing whether it's consistent with or a departure from the rest of your personality — is a starting point, not the whole answer.
