## Is it really the same type, or the same pattern?

It's rarely the physical type doing the work. What usually repeats is one of four measurable relationship patterns — the same emotional unavailability, the same version of being chased, the same difficulty trusting someone means what they say — and Bearing's Relationships branch is built to name which one is yours, not just gesture at "attachment issues" in the abstract.

**A relationship pattern is a consistent filter for who feels compatible once you're actually in something with them — distinct from a physical type, which only affects who catches your attention in the first place.** The type is what you notice. The pattern is what happens next, and it's the part that repeats.

## The four patterns, and what "same type" looks like in each

Bearing's Relationships branch scores two dimensions — anxiety (worry about whether a connection is secure) and avoidance (discomfort with closeness) — and maps the combination to one of four named patterns, not a single spectrum:

- **Open** (low anxiety, low avoidance) — closeness feels natural, not threatening. This is the one group where "why do I keep dating the same type" often doesn't apply the same way, because there's no strong anxious or avoidant pull doing the selecting in the first place.
- **Independent** (low anxiety, high avoidance) — self-reliant by orientation, comfortable alone, not preoccupied with relationships. The repeating pattern here tends to be partners who ask for more closeness than feels comfortable, or a habit of stepping back right as things get serious.
- **Attached** (high anxiety, low avoidance) — wants closeness and doesn't shy away from it, but regularly worries whether the connection is mutual, solid, or enough. The concern isn't distance, it's security — so the repeating pattern tends to be partners who keep that question open rather than settling it.
- **Cautious** (high anxiety, high avoidance) — simultaneously wants connection and keeps distance as protection against it. The repeating pattern is often push-pull: drawn toward someone, then pulling back once it starts to feel real, sometimes with a partner whose own inconsistency confirms the instinct to protect.

These are Bearing's own four categories, not a rebrand of a textbook attachment-style list — and the "same type" you keep running into is usually one of these four doing the selecting, not a coincidence.

## Where this connects to what's actually measured

Two related traits are worth naming, because both get confused with the relationship pattern above even though they're measuring something narrower. [Trust](/blog/trust), an Agreeableness facet, measures how readily you assume good faith in others generally — not specifically in the context of closeness and abandonment. And [Anxiety](/blog/anxiety), a Neuroticism facet, measures how much you worry across situations broadly, not attachment-specific fear of losing a connection. The two pairs often travel together in the same person, but a repeating relationship pattern can show up even in someone who scores as generally trusting and generally low-anxiety elsewhere in the report — which is part of why Bearing scores the Relationships branch as its own thing rather than assuming it from Big Five facets alone.

## Does knowing your pattern actually change anything?

Not by itself, and it's worth being honest about that rather than promising otherwise. Awareness doesn't erase a pattern — what the research on attachment change points to is a relationship that consistently doesn't confirm the old expectation, sustained over real time, or therapy specifically aimed at the pattern itself. What awareness does reliably do is turn a repeating feeling of bad luck into a named, specific pattern — which at minimum stops it from feeling random, and at most gives you something concrete to bring into the next relationship's early conversations.
