**The ECR-R is a 36-question test that gives you two scores: how much you worry about whether a close relationship is safe (anxiety), and how uneasy you feel with closeness (avoidance).** Researchers use it to study adult relationships, and it's the starting point for many attachment quizzes, including Bearing's.

## Where it came from

Before the ECR, researchers measured adult attachment with lots of separate tests, each asking about something slightly different. In 1998, Brennan, Clark and Shaver gathered the questions from dozens of those tests and looked for what they had in common. Almost everything boiled down to two things: anxiety and avoidance. They built a 36-question test around those two scores and called it the ECR.

Two years later, Fraley, Waller and Brennan went through the questions again. They used a method that checks how much each single question tells you, at every level of the score and not just for average people. They kept the two scores and the length, but picked questions that measure people at the high and low ends more accurately. That revised version is the ECR-R. The "R" means a better-tuned test of the same two things, with no new theory behind it.

## How it's scored

The questions are statements like "I worry that a partner won't care about me as much as I care about them," and you rate how much you agree. In the original, the scale runs from 1 to 7. Half of the 36 questions feed the anxiety score and half feed avoidance.

Some statements are worded the other way round, so that agreeing means less anxiety or less avoidance. Those answers get flipped first. Then each score is simply the average of its questions. The result is two numbers, and the test itself doesn't sort people into groups. Labels like "anxious" or "avoidant" come later, when someone chooses where to draw lines on those two numbers.

## Shorter versions

Thirty-six questions is a lot for a quick check, so shorter forms exist:

- **ECR-S** (Wei and colleagues, 2007): 12 questions, 6 per score, taken from the original ECR.
- **ECR-RS** (Fraley and colleagues, 2011): 9 questions that you can answer about one particular person, such as a partner, a parent or a close friend. That makes it possible to see that someone can feel secure with one person and anxious with another.

Shorter versions trade a little accuracy for speed. They're good for a general read, less good for telling apart two people with close scores.

## How Bearing uses it

Bearing's Relationships branch adapts the ECR-R into 15 statements: 7 for anxiety and 8 for avoidance, rated from 1 to 5. Flipped items are reversed, each score is the average of its statements, and the middle of the scale (3) is the dividing line on both. That gives four groups, which Bearing calls Open, Independent, Attached and Cautious. [What the four patterns mean](/blog/attachment-style-quiz) is explained on its own page.

## What it can't do

The ECR-R describes how you tend to feel in close relationships, based on your own answers. It can't diagnose anything, and it can't see what you don't notice about yourself. Your scores can also shift with a new relationship or a hard year, so a result says more about where you are now than about who you'll always be.
