A packed party is either a battery charging or a battery draining, and which one it is has almost nothing to do with how confident someone feels once they're actually there. That's the specific thing this facet measures — not confidence, not charisma, just whether a crowded room is fuel or friction.

Bearing's assessment measures Gregariousness with four items: whether you enjoy being part of a group, whether you like a lot of people around, whether you prefer active, busy settings — and, scored in reverse, whether you'd rather avoid crowds. Notice what's missing: nothing about whether you speak up once you're in the room, and nothing about how close you feel to any one person there. It's a measure of pull toward group settings specifically, not of social skill or social depth.

That's what separates it cleanly from [Assertiveness](/blog/assertiveness). Assertiveness asks whether you take charge once you're in a group. Gregariousness asks whether you wanted to be in the group at all. Someone can be high in Gregariousness and low in Assertiveness — genuinely energized by a full room, perfectly happy to stay in the mix without ever steering it.

## Where this actually shows up

It shows up most clearly in how someone recovers. A high-Gregariousness person who's had a quiet, isolated week often describes feeling flat, even if nothing went wrong — the flatness is specifically the absence of a crowd, not the absence of any particular person. A low-Gregariousness person can have a full, warm week of close one-on-one time and still feel drained by a single large gathering at the end of it, because the drain isn't about the people individually, it's about the density of people at once.

## Context changes what the score means

A high score reads differently depending on what's actually available. In a job with constant open-office bustle, high Gregariousness is a quiet advantage nobody names directly. In a remote, mostly-solo role, the same score can leave someone flat for reasons that have nothing to do with the work itself — the missing ingredient is crowd density, not job satisfaction, and it's easy to misdiagnose one as the other.
